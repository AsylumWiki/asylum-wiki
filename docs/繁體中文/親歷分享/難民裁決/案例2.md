**MEMBER:** These are the reasons behind my decision. [Name Redacted], you are a Convention refugee; your claim is accepted. The summary of your claim: You claim to be a citizen of China. You have asked for Canada's protection. You alleged that you fear persecution in your country because of your gender identity as a transgender woman.

In deciding this claim, I have considered and applied the Chairperson's Guideline 9 on *[Proceedings Before the IRB Involving Sexual Orientation, Gender Identity and Expression, and Sex Characteristics.](https://irb-cisr.gc.ca/en/legal-policy/policies/Pages/GuideDir09.aspx)*

## Identity

You proved who you are and where you are from. You have established your identity on the balance of probabilities. This means I believe it is more likely than not you are who you say you are. You established this through your testimony and documents, specifically by your original Chinese passport which was seized by the Canadian immigration authorities.

## Convention Refugee

Your fear is linked to one of the grounds of persecution in the Refugee Convention. There's a link between what you fear and one of the five (5) protection grounds listed in the Refugee Convention. Specifically, I find that your claim is connected to your gender identity as a transgender under the particular social group.

Since your claim is linked to one of the five (5) protection grounds, I must determine whether you face a serious possibility of persecution in China. You proved important parts of your refugee claim. I find your evidence about your gender identity credible. I find that it is more likely than not what you claim happened to you after you shared your gender identity with your parents and family upon your arrival in Canada did happen.

You submitted extensive medical documentation from Canadian medical professionals and other Canadian support services confirming your diagnoses, suicide attempt, mental condition, transition-related treatment, and ongoing care. Having reviewed this documentation and in the light of the Chairperson's Guidelines, I did not consider it necessary to question you in depth about the details of your transition or the expression of your gender identity.

Some of these documents include your medical birth certificate that you were born as a male, letter from your psychiatrist as proof of your admission after you attempted suicide, letter from your counsellor in relation to your transgender, comprehensive reports from the Department of Youth Protection about your mental condition, diagnoses, treatment, and conclusion that the DYP provided adequate support until you attend the age of majority.

A letter from a medical doctor with prescription of your hormone therapy. A letter from [Name of organization redacted], a nonprofit community organization that provides support to queer youths. A letter from two (2) friends who are aware of the challenges you have been through and who also appeared at the hearing as a witness and as an observer. Pictures of yourself at the parade and proof of your medications.

I have no issues with these documents on the face of them and assign them full weight to find that they establish your claim of being a transgender woman. In these circumstances, I did not also consider it necessary to question you about your sexual orientation as it is not determinative of your claim and would not materially assist the assessment of your alleged risk as a transgender woman.

Based on your evidence before me, I find on the balance of probabilities that you have established a subjective fear of persecution, meaning that you genuinely fear being persecuted for your gender identity as a transgender in China. I find that the credible evidence that goes to the core of your claim outweighs any contrasting evidence. Your fear about what will happen to you in China is objectively well-founded.

What you fear might happen to you if you return to China is supported by objective evidence in the country conditions in the [National Documentation Package for China](https://irb-cisr.gc.ca/en/country-information/ndp/Pages/index.aspx?pid=13000), the version of 31 October 2025. I find that you have a serious possibility of persecution based on your gender identity and expression if you are returned to China. Your past experience with your parents and [Redacted] also demonstrates a prospect of further harm and persecution in the context of China.

The objective evidence at [Tab 6.3](https://wepub.org/index.php/IJSSPA/article/view/3206/3494), the National Documentation Package, a document titled China's LGBT Community, Its History, Current Situation, and Future Prospects state as follows. The historical and cultural perspective on gender and sexual orientation in China has evolved significantly from Asian to modern times. Modern China has seen substantial changes in gender roles due to economic development, social modernization, and government policies promoting gender equality.

Despite these advances, the LGBTQ+ community still faces challenges such as social discrimination and lack of legal protection. The issue of social acceptance and discrimination against the LGBTQ+ community in modern China is complex and multi-layered. In recent years, with societal progress and openness, the LGBTQ community has gained more understanding and support in some large cities and among younger generations. Despite the increased social recognition of LGBTQ+ individuals in some cities, discrimination remains a serious issue.

LGBTQ+ individuals often face clear discrimination and exclusion in areas such as employment, healthcare, and public services. Family pressure is also a significant factor with many LGBTQ+ individuals facing strong opposition and rejection from relatives due to failure to meet traditional family expectations. Additionally, gender stereotypes and negative views on homosexuality in societal culture further exacerbate social discrimination against the  LGBTQ+ community.

In summary, while social acceptance of the LGBTQ+ community in China is gradually increasing, significant social discrimination and prejudice still exist. In China, there is still much room for improvement in the legal protection and social acceptance of the LGBTQ community. Although there has been progress in recent years, the current laws and policies do not fully protect the rights of these groups. First, enacting and implementing specific anti-discrimination laws is an important step in protecting LGBTQ rights, and this has not been done.

Explicitly prohibiting discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity can also provide strong legal protection for the LGBTQ community. Currently, China's legal system lacks specific provisions in these areas, leading to a lack of legal basis for LGBTQ individuals facing discrimination in employment, healthcare, education, and other fields.

Secondly, the process for LGBTQ individuals in China to change gender information on identification document involves cumbersome procedures, often including medical certification and gender reassignment surgery. Improving these procedures to be more humane and respectful of individual gender identity can significantly enhance the quality of life and well-being of LGBTQ+ individuals.

Therefore, there is still much room for improvement in the legal protection and social acceptance of the LGBTQ community in China. I therefore find that you have established an objective basis for your fear.

## State Protection and Internal Flight Alternative

You are not adequately protected by your state or safe anywhere in China. The objective evidence above states that there is no adequate state protection available to you in China, and there is no internal flight alternative because there is nowhere safe for you to go within China that you would not experience exclusion and discrimination.

## My Conclusion

your claim is accepted. Based on all the evidence and my analysis, I determined that [Name] is a Convention refugee because you face a serious possibility of persecution in China. Your claim is therefore accepted. The hearing is now concluded.
